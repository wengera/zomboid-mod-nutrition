## plan-12 niacin UL basis (2026-10-13)

Harvester: claude-opus-5-5. Resolved by reading the National Academies OpenBook text of the record itself (the page HTML fetched and searched; page numbers are the OpenBook "Page N" markers of the printed book).

### Niacin (S1.1, provisional)

Record: Institute of Medicine 1998, *Dietary Reference Intakes for Thiamin, Riboflavin, Niacin, Vitamin B6, Folate, Vitamin B12, Pantothenic Acid, Biotin, and Choline*, National Academies Press, doi:10.17226/6015, Chapter 6 "Niacin", https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/6015/chapter/8 (nap.nationalacademies.org redirects there).

- p. 138, Tolerable Upper Intake Levels > Hazard Identification > Adverse Effects:
  "There is no evidence of adverse effects from the consumption of naturally occurring niacin in foods. Therefore, this review is limited to evidence concerning intake of niacin as a supplement, food fortificant, or pharmacological agent."
- p. 140:
  "The Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) developed here applies to all forms of niacin added to foods or taken as supplements (e.g., immediaterelease, slow or sustained-release nicotinic acid, and niacinamide [nicotinamide])."
- p. 123 (chapter summary), the value S0269 carries:
  "The Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) for niacin for adults is 35 mg/day, which was based on flushing as the critical adverse effect."
- Derivation (Dose-Response Assessment): "A LOAEL of 50 mg/day was divided by a UF of 1.5 to obtain the UL for adults of 35 mg/day, a rounded estimate."

Corroborating, the Food and Nutrition Board's DRI summary table "Dietary Reference Intakes (DRIs): Tolerable Upper Intake Levels, Vitamins" (NASEM; https://www.nationalacademies.org/cdn/materials/9fb9faeb-1faf-41d1-b2f7-381c9b8bdbef, a PDF marked "PREPUBLICATION COPY: UNCORRECTED PROOFS"; sources list the 1998 report), footnote c:
  "The ULs for vitamin E, niacin, and folate apply to synthetic forms obtained from supplements, fortified foods, or a combination of the two."
and its NOTE: "Unless otherwise specified, the UL represents total intake from food, water, and supplements." (niacin is so specified by footnote c).

Linus Pauling Institute niacin page (url:https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/vitamins/niacin): states the 35 mg/d UL and "The UL applies to the general population and is not meant to apply to individuals who are being treated with a nutrient under medical supervision", but does NOT state the supplement/fortificant scope; it also reports NHANES "total usual niacin intakes exceeding the UL", a looser use. NIH ODS fact sheet: fetch refused (HTTP 403, Cloudflare challenge); a search snippet of the ODS consumer sheet paraphrases the same scope, not quoted here as unread.

Resolution: the claim "the IOM niacin UL applies to niacin from supplements, fortified foods or both, not to niacin naturally present in food" is settled against the record (pp. 138, 140) and the FNB table footnote c.

### Folate (S1.2, provisional)

S0330 says "applies to folic acid, not food folate" but neither names supplements and fortified food nor cites the IOM record, so a row is added.

Record: the same report, Chapter 8 "Folate", https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/6015/chapter/10.

- p. 197 (chapter summary): "The Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) for adults is set at 1,000 µg/day of folate from fortified food or as a supplement, exclusive of food folate."
- p. 273, Adverse Effects: "No adverse effects have been associated with the consumption of the amounts of folate normally found in fortified foods (Butterworth and Tamura, 1989). Therefore, this review is limited to evidence concerning intake of supplemented folate."
- p. 280, Derivation of a UL: "The LOAEL of 5 mg/day of folate was divided by a UF of 5 to obtain the UL for adults of 1 mg/day or 1,000 µg/ day of folate from supplements for fortified food." (sic, "for" in the record) and the summary box "UL for Adults 19 years and older 1,000 µg/day of folate from fortified food or supplements".
- p. 197 Background: "Folic acid ... is the form used in vitamin supplements and in fortified food products." and "Most naturally occurring folates, called food folate in this report, are pteroylpolyglutamates".
- FNB summary table footnote c, as quoted above.

Resolution: settled; the IOM folate UL covers folate (folic acid) from supplements and fortified food, exclusive of food folate.
