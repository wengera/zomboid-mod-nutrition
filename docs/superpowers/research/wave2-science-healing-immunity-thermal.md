# Wave 2 science: healing, immunity, bone and thermoregulation

## How to read this

This is the human-science evidence base for the healing, bleeding, infection, bone and thermal effects of the nutrition mod, written for the effects table of [the design spec](../specs/2026-09-27-nutrition-mod-design.md) § 4.5 and constrained by the levers [the jar reading](jar-health-surfaces.md) found.
Sections 1–6 are science and carry no engine content; § 7 and `## Proposed model` are the only speculative parts and are labelled as such.

**Grades**, strongest first: `MA` meta-analysis or systematic review of trials, `RCT` a single randomised trial, `COH` cohort or case-control (including meta-analyses of them, marked `COH-MA`), `AUTH` an authoritative report or guideline (NIH ODS fact sheets, WHO, consensus statements), `TXT` a textbook or narrative review, `CASE` a case report or small case series used only as an order-of-magnitude anchor, `EXP` a controlled human depletion experiment (old, small, and unrepeatable for ethical reasons, but the only direct evidence of deficiency *onset* — treated as strong for timescale and weak for magnitude).

**Every citation in this report was verified** by fetching the bibliographic record from Crossref (`https://api.crossref.org/works/<DOI>`) or Europe PMC (`.../europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:<doi>`) and matching title, authors, year and journal. A citation that could not be verified is not used; where a number is widely repeated but I could not reach a source that states it, the row says `UNVERIFIED — not usable` and the number is excluded from `## Proposed model`. Effect sizes are quoted as the source states them, with its own confidence interval; where I converted a unit the row says so.

**A standing caveat on population.** Almost the entire nutrition-and-outcome literature is drawn from hospital inpatients, older adults, surgical patients, children in low-income countries, and endurance athletes. There is essentially no evidence base for the mod's actual population — healthy adults aged 20–50 under acute, weeks-long, mixed deficiency and physical threat. Every row's population column is therefore load-bearing, and the transfer from that population to a Project Zomboid survivor is a game-design decision, not a scientific inference. § 7 makes that decision explicit rather than hiding it inside a coefficient.

The honest shape of this evidence is that *macronutrient adequacy matters and single micronutrients above adequacy do not.* The Cochrane review's prevention estimate (RR 0.86) sits exactly on the significance boundary and its healing arm is frankly null, so the mod should not model a generic "eat more protein, heal faster" bonus at any large magnitude. What does replicate is the enriched-formula effect: three RCTs and a 200-patient trial agree on roughly a 16–19 percentage-point improvement in wound *area* reduction over eight weeks, but agree just as clearly that complete healing and wound-infection incidence did not move — the supplement changes the slope, not the endpoint, and it was tested only in already-malnourished people against an isocaloric control, so the effect is arginine/zinc/antioxidant-specific rather than an energy effect. That asymmetry is the single most useful fact in this section for a game: **restoring a deficient state gives a real healing gain; exceeding adequacy gives nothing.** The two isolated-nutrient trials are the negative controls that make the point — 1000 mg/d of vitamin C moved pressure-ulcer closure not at all (HR 0.78, interval straddling 1, and the control group actually healed slightly faster), and pooled oral zinc for leg ulcers gave RR 1.22 with a CI through unity. Against that, malnutrition as a *state* roughly doubles to triples infection risk after surgery (OR 2.3–3.4 across two independent meta-analyses of over 175 000 and 9 studies), which is a much larger and much better-evidenced signal than any supplement effect and is the one the mod should carry. Note what this means directionally: the defensible model is a **penalty for deficiency**, not a bonus for surplus. Two things the brief asked for are simply not in the literature — a human effect size for vitamin A on healing rate, and any trial of dehydration on healing — and both are recorded as gaps rather than guessed.

This section produced the clearest negative result of the whole report, and it matters because it contradicts the intuition the design brief starts from. **Dietary vitamin K restriction does not prolong clotting in a healthy adult on any timescale a game cares about.** Two independent metabolic-ward experiments took healthy people to 13–18 % of baseline plasma phylloquinone over 13 days, and to half of baseline over 21 days at 32–40 µg/d for 40 days, and in both the clotting times were flatly unchanged — what moved were the sensitive carboxylation markers (undercarboxylated prothrombin, urinary Gla), which are biochemistry, not bleeding. The NIH ODS fact sheet states the general case outright: reaching an intake low enough to alter standard clinical coagulation measures is "almost impossible" on a varied diet, and adult clinical deficiency is a malabsorption or drug phenomenon. A vitamin-K-driven bleeding-time multiplier is therefore **not defensible as physiology**; if the mod wants one it must be labelled a game stylisation, and the most honest framing is that a Zomboid survivor's diet is not "varied" — months of canned goods with no greens, plus the gut-flora and malabsorption states the game does not model, is outside the population these experiments studied, and the experiments show the *direction* even if not a clinically relevant magnitude.

Vitamin C is the opposite case: capillary fragility, purpura and haemorrhage are *definitional* signs of scurvy rather than a marginal effect, they appear within about a month of near-zero intake, and a 280-case modern synthesis confirms purpura, anaemia and haemorrhage as the dominant presenting phenotype. So the bleeding lever the evidence supports is vitamin C, not vitamin K, and it is a threshold effect at clinical deficiency rather than a graded one — which fits the mod's status ladder well, since it belongs at the `clinical` rung and nowhere above it. Finally, neither omega-3 nor alcohol earns a bleeding term: fish oil measurably reduces platelet aggregation and measurably does *not* increase surgical bleeding across 52 studies, and acute alcohol at 0.5 g/kg had only modest haemorheological effects with a *pro*-aggregatory rebound at 13 hours. Chronic heavy drinking does cause a haemorrhagic diathesis, but through thrombocytopenia and liver-mediated coagulopathy — a different mechanism on a different timescale, and one the mod has no compartment for.

Three of the four "immune vitamins" the brief names have weakened rather than strengthened since their headline papers, and a design written from 2017 citations would be wrong. **Vitamin D is the biggest correction.** Martineau's 2017 individual-participant meta-analysis is still the best-designed study in the field and still gives OR 0.88 overall with a striking OR 0.30 in the severely deficient, but the same group's 2025 update — 40 studies and 61 589 participants, including one trial of 15 804 people alone — returns OR 0.94 with the interval touching 1.00, finds **no** effect modification by baseline vitamin D status, and reports left-sided funnel asymmetry, which is the signature of small-study bias inflating the earlier estimate. The mod can still carry a vitamin D term, but it must be small, it must be justified as a deficiency-correction rather than a supplementation bonus, and the spec's "vitamin D repletion and respiratory infection risk" balanced-diet bonus should be re-graded down or cut. **Vitamin C splits cleanly by population**: null for incidence in the general community (RR 0.97) and about 8–14 % shorter colds, but RR 0.48 — a halving — in marathon runners, skiers and soldiers on subarctic exercises. That subgroup is, almost uncannily, the Project Zomboid survivor: heavy sustained exertion in the cold. It is the one place in this entire report where the evidence population matches the game's, and it is the strongest single justification for a vitamin C term on cold-catching. **Zinc** is real but modest and noisy — 5 prevented infections per 100 person-months, about two days shorter illness at very low certainty, no effect on average severity, and more nausea — and it failed outright when tested against a controlled rhinovirus challenge, so it belongs as a small duration modifier rather than a susceptibility one.

**Vitamin A is the largest clean effect in the section but the least transferable.** Halving measles incidence (RR 0.50) and cutting all-cause child mortality 12–24 % are enormous, and they are measured in over a million children — but they are measured in children aged 6–59 months in vitamin-A-deficient populations, where baseline deficiency is endemic and measles is a mass killer. Nothing licenses transferring that magnitude to a well-grown adult, and the trials found **no** effect on respiratory-disease incidence or mortality, which is the infection class the game actually models. **Iron is the genuine double edge and the most interesting result for a survival game**: routine iron in a high-malaria setting raised serious illness 12 % and hospitalisation 11 % so decisively that the safety board halted those arms, while the same authors state that the iron-deficient and anaemic *do* benefit. That is an inverted-U, not a monotonic benefit, and it is the only nutrient in this report with published harm from supplementing the replete. Finally, **energy deficit and sleep loss are the two best-evidenced immunity levers for the mod's actual population**: 14 days at 22 kcal/kg FFM/day raised cortisol 22 % and nearly doubled a leukocyte oxidant-producing enzyme in healthy young athletes, low energy availability carried OR 3.8 for upper-respiratory illness, and — the cleanest causal design in the whole report — sleeping under 6 hours before a controlled rhinovirus inoculation raised the odds of an actual clinical cold about fourfold. Against that, the "open window" from a single hard effort is now contested enough that the mod should not model acute exertion as immunosuppressive at all; the deficit, not the exercise, is the signal.

This section answers the brief's own question with a clear no. **A fracture-risk multiplier is not defensible on any timescale the mod runs on, and arguably not at all.** Four independent large syntheses agree: calcium alone, vitamin D alone, and the two combined all fail to reduce hip fracture in the JAMA meta-analysis of 33 trials and 51 145 people, with point estimates of 1.53, 1.21 and 1.09 — all *above* unity; Cochrane finds high-quality evidence that vitamin D alone does nothing (RR 1.12 hip, 1.03 any); Bolland's trial sequential analysis of 81 trials and 53 537 participants crosses the futility boundary at a 5 % relative risk, which is the statistical statement that the effect is not merely unproven but bounded below detectability; and VITAL, the single largest trial ever run on this question, gives HR 0.98 over 5.3 years in 25 871 people with no modification by baseline vitamin D status. The one positive result in the whole field is Cochrane's calcium *plus* vitamin D, RR 0.84 for hip fracture — and Cochrane itself translates that into **one fewer hip fracture per 1000 older adults per year** in a community population. Scaled to a month of game time that is a change of roughly eight thousandths of one percent, and it was measured in post-menopausal women and men over 70, not in a 30-year-old survivor.

Protein deserves a note because the folklore runs the wrong way. The National Osteoporosis Foundation's systematic review found higher protein intake *protective* at the lumbar spine (+0.52 % BMD) and, more importantly, stated outright that there are no adverse effects of higher protein intake — so the mod must not implement the old "high protein leaches calcium and weakens bone" idea, which this review retires. The remaining honest statement about timescale is qualitative: vitamin D deficiency below 30 nmol/L causes osteomalacia through defective mineralisation *during remodelling*, and osteoporosis is described as "in part, a long-term effect" of insufficiency — both are remodelling-mediated, and remodelling is the slowest process in this report. So if the design wants any bone consequence at all, the defensible one is not fracture probability but a **flavour-level, non-mechanical** expression at clinical vitamin D or calcium deficiency sustained for months, or nothing. § 7 recommends nothing, and records why that is the scientifically honest choice even though it removes a mechanic the brief asked about.

The engine already reads the two terms the science makes most important, and this section's main practical finding is about the *third*. Vanilla's thermoregulator takes body weight as fatness and hunger, thirst and fatigue as energy and fluid terms, and both of those reads are physiologically correct: subcutaneous fat is named by an authoritative hypothermia review as a determinant of cooling rate, and malnutrition, insufficient fuel, hypoglycaemia and impaired shivering are all listed as causes of hypothermia through decreased heat production. But Toner's dry-suit experiment is the caution that matters for a game about scavenged clothing: with adequate insulation, a 10.6 % vs 18.6 % body-fat difference produced **no significant** core-temperature difference over three hours in 10 °C water. Body fat is a real but *conditional* insulator whose effect is dominated by what the character is wearing — so a fatness term should be scaled down, not up, whenever the character is well dressed, and the mod should not double-count it on top of clothing insulation the engine already computes.

The clearly justified **addition** is iron. Beard's experiment is unusually well suited to the mod: anaemic women held a core temperature 0.2 °C lower and showed about 12 % lower oxygen consumption — the proxy for heat production — after 100 minutes of mild cold than controls matched for body fatness, low thyroid hormones accompanied it, and — the detail that makes it implementable — **iron-depleted but non-anaemic subjects were indistinguishable from controls**. That is a threshold, not a gradient: the cold penalty should appear only at the mod's anaemic grade, not at marginal iron stores, and it should attach to both the temperature term and the heat-production term. Dehydration's heat side is equally well pinned: Montain and Coyle showed core temperature rising in direct linear proportion to a deficit between 1.1 % and 4.2 % of body mass with sweat rate unchanged, and both Cheuvront's review and the ACSM position stand converge on **2 % of body mass** as the threshold where consequences begin — which is exactly the dehydration ruling the design spec already adopted in § 4.4, so the thermal side can reuse that same threshold rather than inventing a second one. The thresholds for the extremes are hard numbers: hypothermia below 35 °C, shivering lost and rewarming impossible in the exhausted, arrest risk below 30 °C in a young adult, and heatstroke above 40 °C with neurological dysfunction, with observed exertional cases running 40–41.6 °C. Those map cleanly onto `CharacterStat.TEMPERATURE`, whose registered range of 20–40 °C the jar reading gives, with the sobering note that the stat's ceiling of 40 °C sits *exactly at* the heatstroke threshold and its floor of 20 °C is four degrees below apparent death — so the engine's range can express the cold tail fully and the hot tail barely at all. Alcohol and iodine are the two weak spots: alcohol's cold effect is real in epidemiology and mechanism (vasodilation, plus hypoglycaemia when drinking replaces food) but I found no quantified cooling-rate multiplier, and for iodine specifically there is no human cold-tolerance effect size at all.

Three of them are genuinely reachable by a scavenger and therefore worth modelling. **Iron is the best candidate**: the UL is only 45 mg/day, GI symptoms begin right at it, 20 mg/kg (about 1400 mg for a 70 kg adult) causes corrosive gut injury, and 60 mg/kg is potentially lethal — and the ODS text supplies a modifier the mod can actually use, namely that the hazard is worse "if food is not taken at the same time", which maps onto a stomach-contents check the design already has. **Selenium is the one plausible food-borne over-intake**: Brazil nuts run 68–91 mcg per nut against a 400 mcg UL (255 mcg by EFSA's 2023 opinion), so five or six nuts a day sits at the limit and a jar of them is a real hazard — and selenosis has wonderfully legible symptoms for a game, opening with garlic breath and a metallic taste before hair and nail loss. **Vitamin A is the classic survival-fiction toxicity** and the numbers support it: the UL is 3000 mcg RAE, acute toxicity needs "more than 100 times the RDA" in one or a few doses, and the acute syndrome — headache, blurred vision, nausea, dizziness, ataxia, progressing to raised intracranial pressure and coma — is exactly a food-sickness-then-poison escalation.

Two things the brief asked for I could not source and have refused to invent. The **zinc single emetic dose** (widely quoted as 225–450 mg) is not stated in the ODS fact sheet and I could not reach a source for it, so the row says unusable and the mod should key zinc excess off the 40 mg UL and the qualitative symptom list instead. Likewise there is **no verified g/kg lethal dose for salt**; what I have is a case anchor — a litre of soy sauce driving serum sodium to 171 mEq/L with an intracranial bleed — which is enough to place a threshold in the game's own units without claiming a physiological constant. Water intoxication is better served: about 8 litres in 3 hours took one man to 120 mEq/L with a seizure, and the severity bands are published (< 135 unremarkable, < 120 neurological, < 110–115 potentially fatal), which gives a three-rung ladder that matches the engine's `POISON` and `FOOD_SICKNESS` stats directly. Note that vitamin K has no UL at all, so the mod must not model vitamin K excess, and vitamin C's 2000 mg UL is high enough that a scavenger could only reach it through supplements.

## 7. Engine mapping (speculative)

**Everything in this section is speculative design, not science.** The science is §§ 1–6; what follows is my proposal for turning it into the levers [the jar reading](jar-health-surfaces.md) proved exist, and every magnitude is a game-design choice that happens to be anchored to a cited number. Each row's `value / equation` is the proposal; its `grade` is the grade of the *evidence the proposal rests on*, not of the proposal itself, and rows whose evidence is absent are marked `stylisation` so a reviewer can find and challenge them. ## Proposed model

Speculative, as § 7. In one page, the set I recommend the effects table carry, in descending order of how well the evidence supports it.

1. **An infection multiplier driven by protein-energy status**, `I ∈ [1.0, 2.3]`, applied to wound-infection progress per minute on parts with open wounds. This is the best-evidenced nutrition consequence in the whole report: two independent meta-analyses over 175 000 and 9 studies put malnutrition at OR 2.31–3.44 for surgical infection. Implement with `setWoundInfectionLevel` plus `syncBodyPart(part, 32768)`; never with `setInfectionGrowthRate`, which is shared with food sickness and zombie fever.
2. **A cold-catching multiplier**, `C ∈ [0.5, 3.0]`, from vitamin C, energy status and fatigue. Vitamin C earns a *bonus* direction here — the one place in this report where the trial population (marathon runners, skiers, soldiers on subarctic exercises, RR 0.48) matches a Zomboid survivor — and fatigue earns the largest penalty, from the cleanest causal design (under 6 h sleep before controlled rhinovirus inoculation, OR 4.24–4.50). Implement through `setCatchACold` accumulation and, at high `C`, `setColdProgressionRate`.
3. **A penalty-only healing multiplier**, `H ∈ [0.5, 1.0]`, from protein-energy, vitamin C and zinc *deficiency*. No bonus above 1.0, because no trial ever tested pushing an adequate person higher. Implement by scaling the four `*HealthAddition` setters and by slowing the per-part wound timers; there is no healing-rate setter in the jar.
4. **A toxicity ladder on `POISON` + `SICKNESS`**, three rungs at 1–2× the UL, the published acute threshold, and the lethal dose. The mechanical prerequisite is easy to miss: the poison drain needs `POISON > 10` **and** `SICKNESS ≥ 0.25`, and it caps at `POISON ≥ 30`. Iron (UL 45 mg/d, acute 20 mg/kg, lethal 60 mg/kg, worse on an empty stomach) and selenium from Brazil nuts (68–91 mcg per nut against a 400 mcg UL) are the two hazards a scavenger can actually reach.
5. **A small bleeding multiplier from vitamin C only**, `B_c ∈ [1.0, 1.25]`, plus a spontaneous-purpura bleeding event at clinical deficiency. Remember that `bleedingTime` appears twice in the damage formula, so `B` multiplies total bleeding damage by about `B²`.
6. **Thermal additions: iron and dehydration.** An iron term at the **anaemic grade only** (a threshold, not a gradient — Beard's iron-depleted non-anaemic subjects behaved exactly like controls), worth about −0.2 °C of core temperature, which requires a −0.4 °C `TEMPERATURE` write **every tick** because the engine lerps halfway. A dehydration term on the heat side above 2 % body mass, rising linearly to a 4 % cap, reusing the spec's existing dehydration threshold.
7. **Nothing for vitamin K bleeding, nothing for fracture risk, nothing for vitamin A on healing, and only a 6 % vitamin D cold term.** Each of these is a deliberate omission with a cited reason, and each removes something the design brief assumed. The vitamin D one is a live correction to the spec: § 4.5's "vitamin D repletion and respiratory infection risk" balanced-diet bonus rests on Martineau 2017, and the same authors' 2025 update of 40 trials and 61 589 participants finds OR 0.94 with the interval touching 1.00 and no effect modification by baseline status.

The unifying principle, and the one I would defend hardest: **model deficiency as a penalty, not adequacy as a bonus.** Every place in this literature where a supplement was given to people who were not deficient, it did nothing (vitamin C for pressure ulcers, zinc for leg ulcers, vitamin D for fractures, calcium for fractures, vitamin D for respiratory infection in the 2025 update) or did harm (iron in a high-malaria population). Every place a large effect appeared, the population was deficient to begin with. A mod built on "penalty below adequate, flat at and above adequate" is both better physiology and better game design, because it makes food a thing you must not neglect rather than a stat to optimise — and it leaves the spec's one explicit game-choice dial, `BalanceBonus`, carrying the whole of the "eating well feels good" fantasy, honestly labelled.

## Gaps

Ordered by how much they would change the design if filled.

- **No effect size for vitamin A on human wound healing.** Everything reachable is rodent, fish, or topical hydrogel. The human literature is the corticosteroid-impaired-healing work, which I did not verify. Until someone verifies it, the vitamin A healing term must stay absent.
- **No trial of dehydration on wound healing.** Mechanistically plausible, entirely unevidenced.
- **The zinc single emetic dose** (commonly cited as 225–450 mg) is not in the ODS fact sheet and I could not reach a source stating it. The original Food and Nutrition Board DRI volume for zinc is the likely source and is on the NCBI Bookshelf; someone with access should check it.
- **No lethal g/kg dose for sodium chloride.** I have a case anchor (1 L of soy sauce → serum sodium 171 mEq/L with an intracranial bleed) but no physiological constant. Forensic toxicology literature exists; the two case reports I found have no abstracts with doses.
- **No quantified alcohol cooling-rate multiplier.** Direction and mechanism are authoritative; magnitude is absent.
- **No human effect size for iodine deficiency on cold tolerance.** The thyroid link is only indirect, through Beard's observation that anaemic subjects had low T4/T3 alongside impaired thermoregulation.
- **No onset day-count quoted from Hodges 1969 or 1971.** Both papers are paywalled and I refused to quote day counts I could not read; the ~1-month figure in this report comes from the ODS fact sheet instead. Getting the primary text would let the mod calibrate the scurvy timeline precisely rather than approximately.
- **No bone-remodelling cycle duration from a verified source.** Would have strengthened the § 4 argument, though it does not change its conclusion.
- **The whole population transfer is unvalidated.** There is no study of healthy adults aged 20–50 under weeks of mixed deficiency plus physical trauma and cold. Every magnitude in § 7 is a transfer from hospital inpatients, older adults, children in low-income countries, or endurance athletes. This is not a fixable gap — it is the permanent epistemic condition of this design, and it is why § 7 states magnitudes as design choices with citations rather than as derived constants.
- **Exercise-induced immune suppression is contested**, so the mod should not model acute exertion as immunosuppressive. If Campbell and Turner's revisionist position is later overturned, an exertion term becomes available.
- **`setKnockedDown` behaviour is a run question**, per the jar reading, and there is no unconscious state in the engine — so hypoglycaemia, severe dehydration and heat syncope cannot express as collapse and must route through fatigue, speed and the temperature stat, as the spec already concluded.
