# Vitamins: evidence base for a realism nutrition model

Scope: vitamins A, D, E, K, C, B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, B7, B9, B12 and choline. Audience: the design of a mod that tracks nutrients beyond the vanilla macros for a character living days to months on scavenged food in Kentucky, 1993.

## How to read this

Every row carries a grade for the kind of evidence behind it:

| grade | meaning |
|---|---|
| MA | meta-analysis or systematic review of human studies |
| RCT | randomised controlled trial |
| COH | cohort, case series, cross-sectional, or a controlled human depletion/repletion experiment (the historical metabolic-ward studies sit here: they are small and unblinded, but they are the only direct measurements of depletion timing that exist) |
| AUTH | authoritative report — IOM/NASEM DRI, EFSA, the Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023 scoping reviews, WHO, USDA |
| TXT | textbook, narrative review, or expert consensus without a systematic search |

Three conventions matter for reading the tables.

**Every citation was verified by fetching its record.** Each was checked against Crossref (`api.crossref.org/works/<DOI>`), the Europe PMC REST API, or PubMed Central, and the title, authors and year were confirmed to match. Where a number could be traced only to a secondary source, the row names the secondary source that was fetched *and* the primary study, and says which one the number came from. Where a number could not be traced to a fetchable record at all, the row says **not verified in this run** and the number is not used to support a design decision.

**Requirement values are quoted from the source that was actually fetched.** The IOM/NASEM DRI reports themselves are paywalled and their summary tables were unreachable (the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements and NCBI Bookshelf both refused automated fetches). So DRI values below were confirmed by fetching either the Linus Pauling Institute Micronutrient Information Center (which reproduces the DRI tables) or the relevant Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023 scoping review (which quotes IOM and EFSA values side by side). The DRI report is cited as the origin; the fetched page is named as the confirmation route. **EARs were mostly not recoverable this way** — the secondary sources quote RDAs and AIs far more often than EARs — so an EAR appears below only where a fetched source stated it. This is a real limitation and it is repeated in `## Gaps`.

**Depletion-study grades are honest, not flattering.** The classic depletion experiments (Herbert on folate, Hodges and Bean on pantothenate, Horwitt and Lane on riboflavin, Goldsmith on niacin, Hodges on vitamin C, Sauberlich on vitamin A) are graded COH. They are the strongest evidence that exists for *timing*, and there will never be better, because the experiments are no longer ethical. They are not graded MA or RCT and should not be read as if they were.

A note on what "deficiency" means here. Most of the modern meta-analytic literature tests *supplementation of already-replete people*, and mostly finds nothing. That is a different question from what happens when intake stops. A game model cares about the second question, so the depletion experiments and the deficiency-disease literature carry more weight in this document than the supplement trials, and the supplement trials are reported mainly to mark where a proposed mechanic is **unsupported**.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Vitamin A is a *slow* store with a *fast, legible* symptom, which makes it the best candidate in the whole set for a visible mechanic: model a hepatic pool that a deficient diet drains over months, and express depletion as degraded night vision before anything else. The DRI's own adequacy criterion is "reserves sufficient for four months", so a store sized at roughly 120 days of turnover is defensible; the exact half-life is not, because it was not recoverable here. Do not model dark adaptation as a sharp switch — the one quantitative human correlation found (pupillary threshold against serum retinol, r = 0.12) is weak, so a gradual penalty with wide individual variation is the honest shape. The mortality and measles effect sizes from Imdad's Cochrane review are real and large, but they come from deficient *children* in endemic areas, so using them as an adult infection multiplier is an extrapolation the evidence does not license; if the model wants an infection term, keep it small and say it is inferred. Vitamin A is also the only vitamin in this list where a single scavenged food can deliver a toxic dose: one 68 g slice of beef liver is 6420 µg RAE against a UL of 3000 µg, so a deer-liver meal is a legitimate acute-toxicity event, and chronic hepatotoxicity has been seen from 7500 µg/d. Carrots and canned pumpkin will carry a player, but at roughly 14% conversion efficiency from whole vegetables, so the model should discount plant sources heavily rather than treating RAE as already-absorbed.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Vitamin D is the one vitamin whose supply in this setting is mostly *not* food, so model it as a sun-exposure stat with a dietary top-up, not as a nutrient in the food table. The kinetics are unusually well pinned: the circulating pool turns over with a ~15-day half-life while the fat-stored parent compound turns over in ~2 months, so a two-compartment store that drains over a season is both physiologically right and mechanically interesting — an indoor, night-playing, basement-dwelling character should drift down over weeks while a farmer does not. Do not model an acute deficiency effect: the bone endpoints (osteomalacia, fracture) develop over months to years and the fracture benefit shows up only alongside calcium, and the supplementation-versus-fracture literature is null for vitamin D alone. The one defensible acute-ish mechanic is infection risk, and Martineau's individual-participant meta-analysis gives an unusually clean shape for it: essentially nothing in replete people (OR 0.88), a large effect in the profoundly deficient (OR 0.58, and OR 0.30 with regular dosing), and nothing at all from big infrequent doses. That maps neatly onto a game rule — deficiency raises infection risk, topping up a replete character does nothing, and a single megadose item should not help. Toxicity is effectively unreachable from food or sun; it needs supplements, and the threshold is a serum level around 750 nmol/L, far above the 100 µg/d UL.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Vitamin E is the strongest "do not model this" finding in the set, and it is worth stating that as a positive result rather than a gap. Roughly 90% of the body's vitamin E sits in adipose tissue, dietary deficiency has never been described in a healthy adult, and over 90% of Americans eat less than the EAR without consequence — so a survival model that makes a character ataxic from a canned-food diet is inventing a disease. If vitamin E appears at all, make it a long-horizon background stat with no symptom, or use it only as a modifier: the one well-evidenced dynamic is that the requirement rises with polyunsaturated fat intake at about 0.5 mg per gram of linoleic acid, which is a genuinely interesting coupling for a diet heavy in vegetable-oil-fried food. The 48–60 hour plasma half-life is tempting to use as a fast store, but it describes only the circulating pool and would misrepresent the body as depleting in days when the real reservoir is fat. The clinical threshold (serum <8 µmol/L, producing neuropathy, ataxia and myopathy) is real but belongs to fat-malabsorption disease states, not to monotonous eating, so if the mod ever models a malabsorption condition, that is where vitamin E earns its place.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Vitamin K has the shortest store and the least interesting consequence, and a model should let those two facts cancel out. Two-thirds of an oral dose is gone within five days and biochemical markers move inside a fortnight of restriction, so on pure kinetics vitamin K is the fastest-depleting vitamin in this document — faster than thiamine. But adult dietary deficiency is described as rare even in the authoritative reviews, the endpoint is a coagulation abnormality rather than a felt symptom, and no UL exists because nobody has documented harm from excess. The honest design is either to omit vitamin K or to model it as a fast-turnover stat whose only effect is a modest bleeding/bruising modifier that stacks with vitamin C rather than standing alone. For a Kentucky scavenger the supply picture is unusually simple: green cabbage at 68 µg per cup is the one commonly available source that is not a leafy green nobody stockpiles, so a cabbage-and-broccoli garden closes the gap entirely while a canned-and-boxed diet leaves it wide open. Because this is the one vitamin where a 15-day restriction study produced a measurable change, it is also the best candidate if the mod ever wants a *short*-timescale nutrient to make the first game week feel nutritionally alive — but the cost of overstating its symptoms is high, because the real-world signal is a lab value, not a wound that will not close.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Vitamin C is the flagship mechanic and the evidence supports making it so, but not at the timescale most designers assume. The body pool is about 1500 mg and controlled withdrawal produced signs at roughly 40 days in metabolic-ward subjects, while a self-experimenting physician took 161 days — so a store worth six weeks to five months, with wide individual variation, is what the literature actually says. Model the store, drain it, and put the first signs at the six-week mark for a character eating nothing but canned goods and grain. The symptom set is the most game-legible in this document: bleeding gums, easy bruising, wounds that will not close, joint pain, and eventually tooth loss — and the collagen mechanism makes a *wound-healing penalty* the single best-justified gameplay effect of any vitamin here. Repletion is fast and dramatic: a whole refugee-camp outbreak improved within one week of treatment, so eating a bowl of strawberries should visibly turn the condition around in days, which is exactly the feedback loop a survival game wants. Two things are **unsupported** and should be resisted. First, a general immunity bonus: Hemilä's Cochrane review is essentially null in the community (RR 0.97) and only finds an effect under extreme physical stress (RR 0.48) — so if the mod wants a cold-resistance term, gate it on heavy exertion or cold exposure and keep it out of the baseline. Second, megadosing: plasma saturates at 100–200 mg/d and above 400 mg/d there is no measured benefit, so a stack of vitamin tablets should do nothing beyond the first one. Supply in this setting is genuinely precarious — strawberries, foraged berries, tomatoes, cabbage and potatoes carry it, canned and cooked versions carry much less, and rice, pasta, crisps, chocolate and peanut butter carry none — which makes scurvy the most plausible deficiency a hoarding player will actually meet.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Thiamine is the best-designed mechanic in this document and a model should lean on it hard. Three properties make it so. The store is small and measured (~30 mg) with a measured whole-body half-life of 9–18 days, giving a defensible two-to-four-week clock — short enough to matter inside a single playthrough, long enough that it is not a hunger bar. The requirement is *energy-scaled* in the EFSA formulation (0.1 mg per MJ), which means a character doing heavy work needs proportionally more, and that is a mechanic no other vitamin here offers as cleanly: chopping wood all week on a rice diet should deplete faster than sitting in a safehouse. And the deficiency syndrome is dramatic and forks in a way a game can use — a cardiac branch (wet beriberi: breathlessness, oedema, rapid heart, and a fulminant form that kills fast) and a neurological branch (dry beriberi's burning feet and weakness, then Wernicke's ataxia and confusion, then Korsakoff's memory loss). Repletion is near-instant, which is unusually forgiving: hours to days, not weeks. That combination — fast onset, dramatic symptoms, instant cure — is exactly the risk/relief loop a survival game wants, and it is well evidenced. Two caveats. The precise number of days from a thiamine-free diet to clinical beriberi was not recoverable here, so derive the timescale from the half-life and the ~0.5 mg/d threshold rather than quoting a day count as fact. And the dietary picture is period-accurate in an ugly way: polished white rice and refined flour are the classic beriberi foods, but US enriched bread and pasta *are* thiamine-fortified (they were in 1993), so a pasta-and-bread diet is protected while a plain-white-rice diet is not. If the mod has spirits, an alcohol multiplier on thiamine turnover is the best-evidenced interaction term in this whole document.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Riboflavin is a low-priority nutrient that earns one good line of flavour and nothing more. There is no discrete body store — the reserve is whatever is bound up in flavoproteins — so a model has no principled store size to use, and the depletion timeline from the classic Elgin experiments was not recoverable here, which means any day count would be invented. What *is* solid is the symptom picture, and it is unusually cosmetic: cracks at the corners of the mouth, a sore inflamed mouth and throat, a greasy dermatitis, and a mild normocytic anaemia. That is a perfect low-stakes visible cue — a "cracked lips and sore mouth" state that tells the player their diet is narrowing without threatening their life. Model it as a short-store, fast-onset, non-lethal condition and do not attach a work-capacity penalty, because none was verified. Two interactions are worth carrying because both are verified and both are counter-intuitive. Riboflavin deficiency impairs iron *utilisation* rather than absorption, so a riboflavin-poor diet can produce an anaemia that iron alone will not fix; and riboflavin status measurably affects a vitamin-A functional test (pupillary threshold went from 87% abnormal to 30% with iron plus riboflavin), so a model that treats night vision as purely vitamin A is over-simplifying. Supply-wise this is the milk-and-eggs vitamin: dairy, eggs, meat and enriched bread carry it, and the light sensitivity is a genuinely fun detail — milk left in the sun loses half its riboflavin in two hours.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Niacin is the vitamin with the cleanest number and the clearest food story, and that makes it the second-best mechanic after thiamine. The number is the 50–60 days from starting a low-niacin, corn-based diet to deficiency signs — a hard, directly measured human figure that a model can use as the store size without any inference. The food story is that niacin is effectively unavoidable if the character eats *any* meat, fish or peanut butter, because those foods carry both niacin itself and the tryptophan that converts to it at 60:1, and tryptophan conversion means a high-protein diet alone covers the requirement. So the design should treat niacin as the deficiency that only a very specific diet produces: maize or corn as the staple with little meat. That is a narrow but real scenario in a Kentucky setting with corn crops, and it is made sharper by the bound-niacin fact — corn's niacin is chemically unavailable unless the corn is lime-treated, which nothing in a 1993 American kitchen does. A model can honour that by giving corn a near-zero effective niacin value while canned tuna at 8.6 mg per serving covers a day's requirement outright. Pellagra's symptoms are the most cinematic in the set (photosensitive dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia, then death) and the fourth D is genuine, so this is one of only two vitamins here — with thiamine — where a lethality path is properly evidenced. Like thiamine, the requirement is energy-scaled in the EFSA formulation, so heavy work should raise it. On the excess side, niacin flush from 30 mg upward is well documented and harmless, which makes it a good candidate for a comic overdose cue if the mod ever adds vitamin pills.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Do not model pantothenic acid, and treat that as a finding rather than an omission. The decisive fact for a survival model is that a metabolic ward could not produce clinical deficiency with diet alone — subjects on a pantothenate-free diet merely became listless, and the headache, insomnia and tingling extremities only appeared once investigators added ω-methylpantothenate, a pantothenate kinase inhibitor with no dietary analogue. A scavenger eating eggs, milk, chicken, potatoes, peanut butter and broccoli is covered many times over, and there is no store size or half-life in the literature to give a depletion clock any principled shape. If B5 appears at all, make it a dietary-diversity bookkeeping entry with no symptom and no penalty.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Vitamin B6 is a middling-priority nutrient with one genuinely good mechanic and one strong "do not model this" finding. The store is large and slow — about 170 mg with 80–90% parked in muscle and a plasma half-life of 25–33 days — so the clock is months, not weeks, and a character has to eat badly for a long time before anything happens; a metabolic ward pushing intake to under 0.05 mg/d for up to four weeks moved lab values and produced no clinical syndrome. The good mechanic is the protein coupling: the requirement is defined per gram of dietary protein (0.016 mg/g), which is the mirror image of thiamine's energy scaling, so a character living on venison and canned tuna needs proportionally more B6 than one living on rice, and a high-meat, low-vegetable diet is where B6 actually gets tight. The deficiency picture is worth using because it is distinctive rather than generic: a microcytic anaemia (PLP is the cofactor for the first step of haem synthesis, so this is a *different* anaemia from folate's and B12's), plus depression and confusion, painful peripheral neuropathy, a sore tongue and a greasy dermatitis. Supply in a 1993 Kentucky kitchen is easy — potatoes alone are 0.61 mg each, and fish, poultry, meat, bread and milk all carry it — so B6 belongs in the same tier as riboflavin: model it if the mod wants dietary-diversity depth, skip it if it wants fewer stats. Two things are **unsupported**. First, treating the low-status disease associations (cardiovascular disease, diabetes, arthritis) as effects of low intake: inflammation itself lowers plasma PLP, so the arrow probably points the other way. Second, any fast timeline — the day count from a B6-free diet to clinical signs was not recoverable, so derive the clock from the half-life and say so. B6 is, however, the one water-soluble vitamin in this document with a real and well-evidenced toxicity: sensory neuropathy scales with dose and duration (48% of women below 50 mg/d for over six months, 77% at 100–200 mg/d), it is not reachable from food, and it recovers fully within six months of stopping — which makes a vitamin-pill hoarder a legitimate, self-inflicted, reversible neuropathy case if the mod ever adds supplements.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Biotin's only game-relevant angle is the raw egg, and the evidence says to model it as a *modifier on eggs* rather than as a nutrient with a store and a clock. The mechanism is clean and unusually well suited to a food model: avidin in raw egg white binds biotin with near-unique affinity and blocks its absorption, and cooking destroys that binding capacity entirely — so a cooked egg is the best biotin source in the game's pantry at 13–25 µg, while a raw one is an anti-nutrient. The magnitude, however, is small and slow. Four replicated controlled studies induced deficiency by feeding undenatured egg white at an avidin dose binding about seven times normal dietary biotin, and after 28 days every subject was still **asymptomatic** — only lab markers had moved. The clinical picture that everyone quotes (alopecia, the scaly rash around eyes, nose and mouth, ataxia, hallucinations) requires "many weeks to years" of raw egg white, or more usually an inborn biotinidase defect, so a player who eats a few raw eggs should get nothing at all. The honest design is a raw-egg penalty that only bites on sustained abuse: a hidden counter that needs weeks of raw eggs as a staple before it produces a cosmetic hair-and-skin condition, with no work-capacity or health penalty, and no benefit from any amount above the AI because no UL and no toxicity exist. Everything else about biotin is a non-mechanic — no store size, no half-life, no validated status biomarker, no EAR, no UL, ordinary intakes already above the AI, and the authoritative review's own verdict that symptomatic deficiency is rare.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Folate is the best-quantified store in this document and it deserves a first-class mechanic. The pool is 5–10 mg against a ~400 µg/d requirement, which is arithmetically about two to four weeks of intake but empirically three to four months to clinical anaemia — so model a store of roughly 100 days and put the first sign at the three-month mark, which also matches the one authoritative depletion statement available (stores gone in three months without supplements). The symptom is a megaloblastic anaemia with fatigue, weakness and breathlessness, and that translates directly into a stamina and carry-weight penalty, which is exactly the kind of slow, cumulative pressure a long game wants — and it is a *different* anaemia from B6's microcytic one, so the mod can legitimately have several anaemia paths with different causes. Mood is worth a cautious line: people with depression do have lower folate (g = −0.24; OR 1.42–1.55), but supplementing already-replete people does nothing short-term (SMD −0.12), so the defensible rule is that *deficiency* carries a mood penalty while eating extra folate above requirement carries no bonus at all. The single most important finding for this setting is the period correction: US folic acid fortification of enriched grain did not begin until January 1998, so the enriched pasta and white rice in the modern USDA table — 153–180 µg DFE per cup, enough to carry a character on its own — must be set to near zero for 1993, which makes folate deficiency a genuinely plausible outcome of a rice-pasta-and-canned-goods diet in a way it would not be today. That leaves legumes and green vegetables as the only real sources, and boiling destroys about half of it (49% retention in spinach, 44% in broccoli) while steaming destroys none — so the mod has a rare, well-evidenced reason to make cooking method matter. Repletion is slow to read on the standard marker (four weeks of 800 µg/d to move RBC folate) but the underlying store refills faster than that, so a week of lentils and greens should visibly lift the condition. Do not model a cardiovascular or cancer effect; those come from supplement trials in replete populations with effects of a few percent, and they are noise at the scale of a survival game.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Vitamin B12 is the clearest case in this document of a nutrient whose real timescale is *longer than the game*, and the honest design decision is to say so rather than to compress it. The store is 2–3 mg against an obligatory loss of 1.4–5.1 µg/d, and because bile-excreted B12 is reabsorbed through an enterohepatic loop, that store is defended rather than merely spent — dividing the verified numbers gives roughly one to six years to depletion, which is longer than almost any Project Zomboid save. So a character who stops eating animal food on day one will not become B12-deficient inside a normal playthrough, and a mod that makes them deficient in a month is inventing physiology. What B12 *is* good for is a long-campaign or hardcore-length mechanic, and an unusually dramatic one: alone among the fourteen nutrients here, its damage can be permanent — the neurological signs (numb and tingling extremities, loss of position sense, gait ataxia, memory loss, psychosis) "may not be reversed with treatment" once established, because the lesion is defective myelin. That justifies a design where B12 is the one nutrient with an irreversible penalty, which is a strong incentive to keep hunting and fishing rather than living on rice. Two mechanics fall straight out of the absorption data. Only about 2 µg can be absorbed from a single meal and fractional absorption collapses with dose (50% of 1 µg, 5% of 25 µg), so a single deer liver cannot top up a year's worth — B12 must be eaten little and often, which is a genuinely interesting constraint and the opposite of vitamin A's hoardable liver store. And biomarkers plateau at 4–10 µg/d with no UL and no toxicity up to 100 µg/d, so excess should simply do nothing. Supply in this setting is easy as long as the player eats animals: beef at 7.0 µg and salmon at 2.4 µg per serving cover a day outright, milk at 1.3 µg and an egg at 0.4 µg contribute, and deer, rabbit and canned fish all qualify — while rice, pasta, crisps, chocolate, peanut butter, potatoes and every foraged plant contribute nothing at all. The one interaction the mod must get right is the folate mask: large folate intakes correct the megaloblastic anaemia without correcting the deficiency, so if the model shows anaemia as the *warning sign* for B12, a vegetable-rich but meat-free diet would silently suppress the warning while the nerve damage accrued — which is both the real physiology and a very good trap.

**Interpretation for a game model.** Choline is the surprise of this document: it has the fastest measured route from a bad diet to real organ damage of anything in the set, and the damage is mechanically perfect for a survival game. Three weeks on a choline-devoid diet produces liver and muscle dysfunction — a 30% fall in plasma choline, depleted liver stores and a rising ALT at 13 mg/d — and on a very-low-choline diet 77% of men and 80% of postmenopausal women developed fatty liver, liver damage or muscle damage inside 42 days. Muscle damage translates directly into a strength and endurance penalty with no inferential leap, which makes choline a better-evidenced fatigue mechanic than anything the B vitamins offer, and a three-week clock is short enough to bite inside a normal playthrough. Two properties sharpen the design. First, the store has no measured mass but is clearly small and liver-resident, and the circulating pool clears within about eight hours of a meal, so this is a nutrient that needs regular intake rather than a hoardable reserve. Second, susceptibility genuinely varies — oestrogen drives endogenous synthesis through PEMT, so premenopausal women were roughly half as likely to be damaged (44% versus 77–80%), and common PEMT variants raise the requirement; that is a legitimate, evidenced basis for a character trait if the mod wants one. Supply is the reason choline deserves a slot: an egg carries 151 mg and beef, cod, canned salmon and chicken all sit near 71 mg per serving, while the AI is 425–550 mg/d and most Americans already eat less than that — so a character on rice, pasta, crisps and canned vegetables is in deficit almost immediately, and eggs and milk are the cheapest fix. Repletion resolves the damage, so this should be reversible. Two things are **unsupported**: any cognitive or brain-development effect (the review says plainly that no RCTs exist) and any cardiovascular harm from dietary choline (the TMAO rise comes from free choline salts, not food phosphatidylcholine, and the review rejects TMAO as a basis for the UL). Toxicity needs 7.5 g/d, which is unreachable from food, and its signature is a fishy body odour — a good comic cue if the mod ever adds supplements.

**Interpretation for a game model.** The interactions are where a nutrition mod earns its realism, because they are the part players cannot get from a wiki table, and four of them are strong enough to be worth implementing before any second-tier vitamin. **Fat as a gate on the fat-soluble four** is the most important: with fat-free dressing, carotenoid appearance in chylomicrons was *negligible*, and a fat-containing meal raised peak plasma vitamin D3 by 32%, so a character eating boiled cabbage and plain carrots with no oil, butter or fatty meat should get close to nothing from them — which turns cooking oil, lard, butter, peanut butter and fatty fish from calorie sources into absorption enablers, and makes the choice of fat matter (olive-type oil beat coconut by 55% at equal grams). **Vitamin C as an iron multiplier** is the second: the per-mg slope is small (0.0065 percentage points of absorption per mg ascorbate) but non-haem iron absorption starts at a few percent, so a serving of strawberries or tomatoes alongside beans or greens is a large relative gain, while the polyphenols and phytate in those same beans cut absorption by 14–45% — a legitimate reason for the model to score meals rather than foods. **Tryptophan-to-niacin at 60:1** should be implemented as a substitution rather than a bonus: any meat, fish or peanut butter covers niacin twice over, which is precisely why pellagra needs a maize staple with the niacin chemically bound and no meat. And **the folate–B12 mask** is the one interaction with a designed-in trap: because folate and B12 produce an indistinguishable megaloblastic anaemia and raise the same homocysteine marker, a folate-rich, meat-free diet silently suppresses the only visible warning of a B12 deficiency whose neurological damage may be permanent. Two energy- and macro-coupled requirements — thiamine at 0.1 mg/MJ and niacin at 1.6 mg NE/MJ — mean heavy labour should raise B-vitamin drain, alcohol should raise thiamine drain specifically (it blocks the transporter rather than just displacing food), and vitamin E's requirement should rise with polyunsaturated fat at about 0.5 mg per gram of linoleic acid. On processing, the honest summary is that cooking method matters far less than most food models assume, with three exceptions that are well measured: vitamin C and folate are destroyed by boiling and spared by steaming (folate retention 44–49% boiled versus no significant loss steamed), thiamine is unpredictable across a 20–80% range, and anything poured down the drain takes the water-soluble vitamins with it (≥70% of folate and B12 lost to decanted cooking water). Niacin, biotin and pantothenate survive cooking, B12 survives everything but long braising (−25%), carotenoids are *improved* by cooking, and for vitamins D, E, K, B6 and choline no retention fraction could be verified — so those five should be modelled with no cooking penalty rather than a guessed one.

## Gaps

What was looked for across all fourteen nutrients and could not be verified to the standard this document sets. Every item below is a row that says "not verified in this run" somewhere above; nothing here was used to support a design decision.

**Access failures that shaped the whole document.** The IOM/NASEM DRI reports themselves are paywalled, and both the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements and the NCBI Bookshelf refused automated fetches. Every DRI value above therefore reached this document through a secondary source that *was* fetched — the Linus Pauling Institute Micronutrient Information Center, or the relevant Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023 scoping review — with the DRI report named as the origin and the fetched page named as the confirmation route. USDA FoodData Central was rate-limited, so every per-serving food figure above comes from the LPI tables, which reproduce USDA values but are one step removed. Web search was exhausted partway through this run, after which the remaining literature was located through the Europe PMC REST search API and direct fetches only; that route is good at finding records by title and author and poor at finding a number nobody put in a title, which is the main reason the gaps below cluster on *timings*.

**EARs are largely missing.** An EAR was verified for only two nutrients — vitamin E (12 mg/d) and niacin (12 mg NE/d men, 11 mg NE/d women). For vitamins A, B6, folate and B12 the fetched sources quoted RDAs, AIs and EFSA average requirements but not the IOM EAR, so those rows say so. For pantothenic acid, biotin and choline no EAR exists at all — the DRI is an AI precisely because the evidence to set an EAR is absent, and EFSA said explicitly that average requirements "could not be derived" for choline. Vitamin K likewise has no EAR or RDA. This matters for a model that wants to distinguish "below the average requirement" from "below the recommended intake", and it cannot currently be done for six of the fourteen.

**Depletion timings — the largest gap, and the one that matters most.** A direct, measured time from a deficient diet to a clinical sign was verified for only five nutrients: vitamin C (~40 days in controlled withdrawal, 161 days in a self-experiment), niacin (50–60 days on a low-niacin corn diet), choline (3 weeks to liver and muscle dysfunction; 77–80% of men and postmenopausal women damaged within 42 days), folate (~4 months to megaloblastic anaemia, stores gone in 3 months), and biotin (28 days of avidin feeding, producing an explicitly *asymptomatic* biochemical deficiency). For the rest the clock had to be derived from a half-life or a store-to-loss ratio, and each of those rows says so:
- **Vitamin A** — the hepatic-store half-life was not recoverable. The Iowa depletion study record is verified (Sauberlich HE et al 1974, DOI 10.1016/s0083-6729(08)60015-1, PMID 4617402) but its numbers were not fetchable, so the 4-month figure rests on the DRI's adequacy criterion rather than a measured decay.
- **Vitamin E** — no human dietary-depletion timeline exists to recover, because dietary deficiency has never been described in a healthy adult.
- **Vitamin K** — Ferland's demonstration that subclinical deficiency is inducible by diet is verified as a record (DOI 10.1172/JCI116386) but its timings were not fetchable; only the 15-day restriction result (a ~20% fall in urinary metabolites) is quoted.
- **Vitamin C** — the Iowa experimental-scurvy programme records are verified (Hodges RE et al 1971, DOI 10.1093/ajcn/24.4.432; Baker EM et al 1969, PMID 4891320) but the per-sign sequence and its timings were not fetchable, so the order of scurvy signs above is textbook, not primary.
- **Thiamine** — no day count from a thiamine-free diet to clinical beriberi was recoverable; the 9–18 day whole-body half-life and the ~0.5 mg/d threshold are what the clock rests on.
- **Riboflavin** — the Elgin depletion programme and the galactoflavin induction study are verified as records (Horwitt MK, Hills OW 1949, DOI 10.1093/jn/39.3.357; Hills OW et al 1951, DOI 10.1001/archinte.1951.03810050058006; Lane M et al 1964, DOI 10.1172/jci104921) but none of their timelines was fetchable.
- **Vitamin B6** — the classic deoxypyridoxine-antagonist deficiency experiments were not fetchable with timings. Kretsch's 11–28 day depletion at <0.05 mg/d is verified but produced biochemical change only.
- **Folate** — Herbert's 1962 self-depletion experiment is the origin of the classic week-by-week sequence (falling serum folate → hypersegmented neutrophils → macro-ovalocytosis → megaloblastic marrow → anaemia); neither the 1962 paper nor his 1987 restatement (DOI 10.1002/ajh.2830260211, PMID 3310615) was fetchable with the week numbers, so only the aggregate "approximately four months" is used.
- **Vitamin B12** — no source stated a store duration directly. The 1–6 year figure above is explicitly labelled as arithmetic done here from a verified 2–3 mg store and a verified 1.4–5.1 µg/d loss, not as a measurement.

**Store sizes and half-lives that could not be pinned.** Verified store masses exist for thiamine (~30 mg), vitamin B6 (~170 mg, 80–90% in muscle), folate (5–10 mg), vitamin B12 (2–3 mg), vitamin C (~1500 mg body pool) and vitamin A (as a liver *concentration*, 0.1–0.7 µmol/g, rather than a mass). No total-mass figure was recoverable for vitamin K (described only as "relatively low compared to other fat-soluble vitamins"), riboflavin (there is no discrete store — the reserve is whatever is bound in flavoproteins), pantothenic acid, biotin, or choline (the liver store is described qualitatively). Vitamin E's store is known by distribution (~90% in adipose) but not by turnover: the 48–60 hour figure is the plasma pool only and would badly misrepresent the body as depleting in days. Vitamin A's 70–90%-in-liver split is verified but its decay rate is not.

**Cooking, canning and processing losses.** Retention fractions were verified for vitamin C (qualitatively — least stable), thiamine (20–80%), riboflavin (intermediate, plus the 50%-in-two-hours sunlight figure for milk), niacin/biotin/pantothenate ("quite stable"), folate (44–49% retention boiled, no significant loss steamed), B12 (−25% braised, −5.5% deep-fried beef), and carotenoids (cooking *raises* availability). No retention fraction was recoverable for vitamins **D, E, K, B6 or choline**, and no single canning fraction was recoverable for any nutrient — a real gap for a game whose pantry is mostly canned, since canning combines heat, time and a discarded or retained liquid, and the leaching result above (≥70% of folate and B12 into decanted water) suggests the liquid matters more than the heat.

**Per-food values.** USDA FoodData Central was unreachable, so all food figures are LPI's reproductions of USDA values. No per-food table was recoverable for **vitamin D** at all. Biotin's food values carry ranges spanning two orders of magnitude (whole-wheat bread 0.02–6 µg/slice) because food biotin assays are poor, and they should not be treated as precise. Nothing game-specific was verified for venison, rabbit, or wild-foraged berries and greens — every meat and fish figure above is beef, pork, chicken, turkey, salmon, trout, tuna, clams or cod, and extending them to deer and rabbit is an inference the mod should mark as such.

**Claims deliberately not pursued.** Toxicity dose figures for acute vitamin A (the signs are verified, the doses are not). The IOM UL for vitamin E (1000 mg/d) — only EFSA's 300 mg/d supplemental level was confirmed. Any effect size for vitamin C on wound healing in humans (the collagen mechanism is verified, a magnitude is not). Any interaction magnitude between choline, folate and B12 as methyl donors (the shared pathway is verified, the coupling strength is not). Cardiovascular, cancer and cognitive endpoints were read only far enough to establish that they are small, contested, or derived from supplementing replete people, and are marked unsupported rather than quantified.

**Structural limits of this evidence base.** Three should be stated plainly. Most of the direct depletion evidence is 1940s–1970s metabolic-ward work on small numbers of men, often prisoners, and it will never be replaced because the experiments are no longer ethical — it is graded COH throughout and should not be read as trial evidence. Most of the modern meta-analytic evidence tests supplementation of already-replete people and mostly finds nothing, which is a different question from what happens when intake stops. And every requirement above is set for a sedentary, healthy, non-pregnant adult in a temperate climate with medical care; a character doing heavy labour, bleeding, febrile, cold, or injured has requirements this literature does not describe, and the only two requirements that scale with anything the game already tracks are thiamine and niacin (with energy) and vitamin B6 (with protein).
